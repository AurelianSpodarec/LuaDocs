Lua ships no regular-expression engine. What it has instead are *patterns* — ordinary
strings, written to a much smaller notation, that
[`string.find()`](/docs/standard-library/string/find),
[`string.match()`](/docs/standard-library/string/match),
[`string.gmatch()`](/docs/standard-library/string/gmatch) and
[`string.gsub()`](/docs/standard-library/string/gsub) read as a description of the text
to look for. Nothing marks a string as a pattern; it becomes one by being handed to one
of those four.

<RunnableExample code={`local log_line = "2026-08-05 disk full"\nlocal year, month, day = string.match(log_line, "(%d+)-(%d+)-(%d+)")\nprint(year, month, day)\n-- Expected output: 2026\t08\t05`} />

## Character classes

A class stands for a set of characters and matches exactly one of them.

| Class | Matches |
|---|---|
| `.` | any character at all |
| `%a` | a letter |
| `%l` | a lower-case letter |
| `%u` | an upper-case letter |
| `%d` | a digit |
| `%x` | a hexadecimal digit |
| `%w` | a letter or a digit |
| `%s` | a space character |
| `%p` | a punctuation character |
| `%g` | any printable character except a space |
| `%c` | a control character |

Every `%` class above has an upper-case twin that matches everything the lower-case one
does not: `%D` is any character that is not a digit, `%S` anything that is not a space.
`.` is the exception, having nothing left over to complement.

Which characters count as letters, as spaces or as punctuation is settled by the locale
the interpreter runs under rather than by Lua, so `%a` and `[a-z]` are not
interchangeable even for text that looks like plain English.

<Note>
`%` is the escape character, not `\`. The characters that need escaping — the magic ones
— are `^`, `$`, `(`, `)`, `%`, `.`, `[`, `]`, `*`, `+`, `-` and `?`, so a literal full
stop is `%.` and a literal per cent sign is `%%`. Any non-alphanumeric character may
carry a `%` and stand for itself, magic or not, which means escaping punctuation you are
unsure about is always safe.
</Note>

## Sets

Square brackets build a class of your own out of the pieces above. `[aeiou]` is a vowel,
`[%a_]` a letter or an underscore, and a `-` between two characters makes a range with
its low end written first, as in `[0-9]`. A `^` immediately after the opening bracket
negates the whole set: `[^,]` is anything but a comma.

Two characters have to be placed rather than simply typed. A closing bracket goes first
in the set, as in `[]]`, and a hyphen goes first or last, as in `[-+]`. Escaping them
with `%` works just as well. Combining a range with a class — `[%a-z]` — means nothing
in particular, and Lua does not define what it does.

## Repetition

A quantifier written after a class matches a run of it.

| Quantifier | Matches |
|---|---|
| `*` | zero or more, as many as possible |
| `+` | one or more, as many as possible |
| `-` | zero or more, as *few* as possible |
| `?` | zero or one, taking the one where it can |

A quantifier reaches back over exactly one class and no further. There is no bracket that
groups several of them together, so a run of more than one character cannot be repeated.

## Anchors

`^` as the very first character of a pattern pins the match to the start of the subject,
and `$` as the very last pins it to the end; a pattern carrying both has to account for
the whole string. In any other position the two are literal characters — a `$` in the
middle is a dollar sign, and a `^` is only a negation when it follows an opening square
bracket.

[`string.gmatch()`](/docs/standard-library/string/gmatch) is the one exception, and it
applies to `^` alone: a leading caret does not anchor there, because an iterator that
could only ever match at the start of the subject would have nowhere to go after its
first turn. The other three functions all honour it.

## Captures

Parentheses mark the parts of a match you want back. They are numbered by their opening
parenthesis, read left to right, so a capture nested inside another is numbered second:
in `"(%a+(%d+))"` the whole run is capture 1 and the digits within it capture 2.
`string.match()` returns them in order, `string.gmatch()` yields them on each turn, and
`string.gsub()` offers them to its replacement as `%1` through `%9`.

The same `%1` to `%9` written *inside* a pattern are back-references, matching the text
the capture of that number matched rather than anything new — `"(%a)%1"` finds a doubled
letter.

An empty pair of parentheses is a *position capture*. It hands back a number instead of
text: the index of the subject at that point in the pattern. `"()world()"` against
`"hello world"` gives 7 and 12, where the word starts and the index one past where it
ends.

## Balanced and frontier items

Two items look wider than a single character.

`%bxy` matches from an `x` to the `y` that balances it, counting a `+1` for each `x` and
a `-1` for each `y` and stopping at the first place the count falls back to zero. `%b()`
therefore takes a parenthesised expression whole, however deeply the parentheses inside
it nest. The two characters have to be different ones: a delimiter that opens and closes
alike, as a quotation mark does, is outside what `%b` is defined for.

`%f[set]` is a *frontier*, and matches the empty string at a boundary rather than
matching any character: the character before the boundary must be outside the set and the
character after it inside. Past either end of the subject there is no character to
consult, so a zero byte stands in for one. That stand-in is what makes the two ends of a
subject count as boundaries like any other: `%f[%a]` fires before a word that opens the
subject, because the zero byte in front of it is not a letter and the letter after it is,
and `cat%f[%A]` fires at the end of `"cat"` for the mirror reason — `t` is a letter and so
outside `%A`, while the zero byte after it is not a letter and so inside. Frontiers are
how a pattern gets word boundaries, which the notation otherwise lacks, and it is that
treatment of the ends that lets a pair of them match a word sitting flush against either
edge of the subject rather than only one with spaces around it.

## Examples

### Trimming whitespace from both ends

<RunnableExample code={`local padded_name = "   Ada Lovelace   "\nlocal trimmed_name = string.match(padded_name, "^%s*(.-)%s*$")\nprint("[" .. trimmed_name .. "]")\n-- Expected output: [Ada Lovelace]`} />

### Splitting a line into fields

<RunnableExample code={`local ingredient_line = "flour,eggs,butter"\nfor ingredient in string.gmatch(ingredient_line, "[^,]+") do\n  print(ingredient)\nend\n-- Expected output:\n-- flour\n-- eggs\n-- butter`} />

### Replacing whole words only

<RunnableExample code={`local shopping_note = "the cat scattered the catalogue"\nlocal corrected, match_count = string.gsub(shopping_note, "%f[%a]cat%f[%A]", "dog")\nprint(corrected)\nprint(match_count)\n-- Expected output:\n-- the dog scattered the catalogue\n-- 1`} />

The two frontiers demand a boundary on each side of `cat`, which is why `scattered` and
`catalogue` are left alone.

## Gotchas

<Gotcha title="These are not regular expressions">
There is no alternation — no `|` — and no grouping. `(ab)+` does not mean one or more
`ab`: the parentheses are a capture rather than a group, so the `+` has no class in front
of it to repeat and nothing repeats. What a quantifier left dangling like that then means
is not something the notation defines, and it is not worth relying on either way. Where a
regular expression would alternate, match twice and take whichever succeeded, or reach
for a real regex library.
</Gotcha>

<Gotcha title="Outside a set, - is lazy rather than a range">
`-` is the lazy quantifier, taking as few characters as it can get away with, and is the
counterpart of `*` rather than a subtraction or a range. Ranges live only inside square
brackets. Its shortest-run appetite is exactly what makes `"^%s*(.-)%s*$"` trim rather
than swallow: a `.*` in the same place would eat the trailing spaces the `%s*` was meant
to drop.
</Gotcha>
