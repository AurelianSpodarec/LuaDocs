The hyperbolic cosine: the average of *e* raised to the argument and *e* raised to its
negation. Nothing in the library replaced it, so the way to write it now is the definition
itself — `(math.exp(x) + math.exp(-x)) / 2` — and the section below is the one place on the
site that sets out what a hand-built hyperbolic function does and does not inherit from the
original.

<RunnableExample usesEntry={false} code={`local cable_constant = 40\nlocal horizontal_offset = 25\nlocal scaled_offset = horizontal_offset / cable_constant\n-- math.cosh(scaled_offset) produced this value in one call\nlocal hyperbolic_cosine = (math.exp(scaled_offset) + math.exp(-scaled_offset)) / 2\nprint(string.format("%.4f", cable_constant * (hyperbolic_cosine - 1)))\n-- Expected output: 8.0701`} />

<Note>
Nothing on this page calls the function. The examples run on a Lua that does not provide it,
so they build the value out of [`math.exp()`](/docs/standard-library/math/exp) and name the
old call in a comment beside it. The call itself is in the syntax below.
</Note>

## Syntax

```lua
math.cosh (x)
```

<Parameters>
  <Param name="x">The argument, in the same units the exponential takes — a plain number, not an angle.</Param>
</Parameters>

<Returns>
  <Return type="float">The hyperbolic cosine of `x`. A float whatever `x` was. The curve is at its lowest at an argument of zero, where the answer is `1`, and climbs away from there in both directions.</Return>
</Returns>

<Errors>

- Raises when `x` is neither a number nor a string that reads as one, including when it is left out.

</Errors>

## Description

Despite the name this has nothing to do with angles. The circular cosine traces a circle; this
traces a hyperbola, and where the circular cosine repeats every turn and stays inside a fixed
range, this one climbs away from its lowest point without limit in both directions. The shape
is the one a chain or a cable takes when it hangs under its own
weight — the *catenary* — which is why the lead example measures a sag, and it turns up
wherever a symmetric growth in both directions is being described.

Two properties fall straight out of the definition and are worth having in mind. It is
*even*: negating the argument leaves the answer alone, because negating it only swaps the two
exponentials being averaged. And its smallest value is `1`, at an argument of zero, where the
two exponentials are both `1`. From there it climbs in both directions, roughly halving the
larger exponential once the argument is far enough from zero for the smaller one to stop
mattering.

The answer is a float, and stays one at an argument of zero where it lands exactly on `1`.
Nothing here rounds or mirrors its argument, so the library's
[subtype rule](/docs/standard-library/math/floor#which-subtype-comes-back) does not reach
this call.

## Rebuilding a hyperbolic function by hand

The three hyperbolic functions are each an arrangement of two exponentials, and none of them
was replaced by anything in the library. The way back is to write the definition out:

```lua
local hyperbolic_cosine  = (math.exp(x) + math.exp(-x)) / 2
local hyperbolic_sine    = (math.exp(x) - math.exp(-x)) / 2
local hyperbolic_tangent = (math.exp(x) - math.exp(-x)) / (math.exp(x) + math.exp(-x))
```

Each of those is the right value in the mathematics and an arrangement of floating-point
operations in practice, and the two are not the same thing. A hand-built version inherits the
limits of [`math.exp()`](/docs/standard-library/math/exp) and adds arithmetic of its own, so
it can go wrong in two ways that a single library call does not:

- **It can overflow earlier than the function it replaces.** `math.exp` runs out of float
  somewhere past an argument of `709`, and the hyperbolic cosine and sine are about *half*
  the exponential — so their own answers are still comfortably inside the float range at
  arguments where the intermediate `math.exp(x)` has already become an infinity. The division
  by two comes after the overflow, and there is nothing left to divide.
- **It can lose precision where the two terms nearly cancel.** Each exponential is rounded to
  a float before anything is done with it, and that rounding is negligible beside the value it
  sits in. Subtract two such values that nearly agree and the digits they share disappear,
  leaving an answer made largely of the rounding that was already there — a small absolute
  error against a now much smaller result. The subtraction is not at fault; it exposes error
  that the two exponentials carried in. This is the sine's problem rather than the cosine's,
  because the cosine *adds* its two terms, and adding two positive numbers cancels nothing.

Both are quiet. Neither raises, and neither leaves a mark on a result that is otherwise the
right shape. Where an argument stays modest — say inside a few tens — the definitions above
are indistinguishable from the calls they replace, and that covers most of what these
functions are used for. Where an argument can run large or shrink towards zero, the entries
for [`math.sinh()`](/docs/standard-library/math/sinh) and
[`math.tanh()`](/docs/standard-library/math/tanh) carry the specific rearrangement each of
them wants.

For this function the first limit applies and the second does not. Adding two positive
exponentials is as accurate as the exponentials themselves, so a hand-built hyperbolic cosine
is faithful from zero right up to the point where `math.exp` gives out — and then it hands
back an infinity a step or two before the real answer would have needed one.

## Examples

### The sag of a hanging cable

<RunnableExample usesEntry={false} code={`local cable_constant = 40\nlocal spans = { 10, 25, 40 }\nfor index = 1, #spans do\n  local scaled_offset = spans[index] / cable_constant\n  -- math.cosh(scaled_offset) is what this expression stands in for\n  local hyperbolic_cosine = (math.exp(scaled_offset) + math.exp(-scaled_offset)) / 2\n  print(string.format("%d m out: %.4f m of sag", spans[index], cable_constant * (hyperbolic_cosine - 1)))\nend\n-- Expected output:\n-- 10 m out: 1.2565 m of sag\n-- 25 m out: 8.0701 m of sag\n-- 40 m out: 21.7232 m of sag`} />

The sag grows faster than the distance does, which is the whole character of the curve: near
the middle it is almost flat, and it steepens without limit towards the anchors.

### The value at zero, and the symmetry around it

<RunnableExample usesEntry={false} code={`local scaled_offset = 1.25\nlocal rising = (math.exp(scaled_offset) + math.exp(-scaled_offset)) / 2\nlocal falling = (math.exp(-scaled_offset) + math.exp(scaled_offset)) / 2\n-- math.cosh(scaled_offset) and math.cosh(-scaled_offset) agreed the same way\nprint((math.exp(0) + math.exp(-0)) / 2 == 1)\nprint(rising == falling)\n-- Expected output:\n-- true\n-- true`} />

The minimum is exactly `1` rather than approximately `1`, and the two sides of it are the
same value rather than nearly the same. Both come from the definition being a sum of two terms
that simply trade places when the argument's sign flips.

### Where the hand-built version gives out

<RunnableExample usesEntry={false} code={`local far_out = 710\nlocal averaged = (math.exp(far_out) + math.exp(-far_out)) / 2\n-- math.cosh(far_out) still had a finite answer here\nprint(averaged == math.huge)\nprint(math.exp(far_out - math.log(2)) < math.huge)\n-- Expected output:\n-- true\n-- true`} />

The first line overflows: `math.exp(710)` is already an infinity, and halving an infinity
leaves one. The second line computes the same quantity as *e* raised to the argument less the
logarithm of two — subtracting before the exponential rather than dividing after it — and
comes back finite. That is the rearrangement to reach for when the argument can run large.

## Gotchas

<Gotcha title="The hand-built version overflows before the function did">
Halving comes after the exponential, so the intermediate value is twice the answer and reaches
the top of the float range first. The symptom is an infinity where a large but finite number
was expected, at an argument the real function would have handled. Nothing raises and nothing
is marked, and the infinity spreads: multiplied by zero it becomes a not-a-number value,
divided into something it becomes a plain, plausible zero. Scaling before the exponential
rather than after it — the third example — moves the edge back out.
</Gotcha>

<Gotcha title="This is not a cosine of an angle">
The argument is a plain number, not an angle, and feeding this function radians in the
expectation of something bounded between `-1` and `1` gives a number that grows without limit
instead. The shared spelling is a historical accident of the algebra rather than a hint that
the two are interchangeable.
</Gotcha>
