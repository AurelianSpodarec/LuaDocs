Hands back the cosine of an angle. The angle is read as a measurement in *radians*, never in
degrees, and the answer is a float somewhere between minus one and one.

<RunnableExample code={`local cosine_of_sixty = math.cos(math.rad(60))\nprint(cosine_of_sixty)\n-- Expected output: 0.5`} />

## Syntax

```lua
math.cos(x)
```

<Parameters>
  <Param name="x">The angle to take the cosine of, in radians.</Param>
</Parameters>

<Returns>
  <Return type="float">The cosine of `x`, between `-1` and `1`. Always a float, whatever `x` was.</Return>
</Returns>

<Errors>

- Raises when `x` is neither a number nor a string that reads as one.

</Errors>

## Description

The argument is an angle in radians, so a number of degrees must be converted on the way in
with [`math.rad()`](/docs/standard-library/math/rad). A full turn is `2 * math.pi` radians,
not `360`, and a degree measure passed straight through is not an error — it is a different
angle, answered correctly and uselessly.

Where [`math.sin()`](/docs/standard-library/math/sin) measures how far a turn has taken you
up, this measures how far it has taken you across: on a circle of radius one, the cosine is
the horizontal offset. That is why the pair are almost always written together, and why
multiplying both by a radius turns an angle into a position.

Two properties follow from that and are worth having to hand. The cosine starts at one
rather than at zero, so an angle of nothing gives a full-strength result — which is what makes
it the natural weight for "how much of this points the same way as that". And it ignores the
sign of its argument: turning thirty degrees one way and thirty the other gives the same
cosine, where the sines are opposites.

The result is always a float, whatever went in. This function neither mirrors its argument
nor rounds anything, so the library's
[subtype rule](/docs/standard-library/math/floor#which-subtype-comes-back) does not reach it.

The tidy values are no safer to compare against here, for the reason
[set out on `math.sin()`](/docs/standard-library/math/sin#why-the-familiar-values-are-not-exact):
a quarter turn is `math.pi / 2`, `math.pi` is not exactly pi, and the cosine there comes out
as a very small number rather than as zero. Going the other way — an angle back out of a
cosine — is [`math.acos()`](/docs/standard-library/math/acos).

## Examples

### How far a ramp reaches along the ground

<RunnableExample code={`local ramp_length = 4.6\nlocal ramp_angle = math.rad(18)\nprint(string.format("%.3f", ramp_length * math.cos(ramp_angle)))\n-- Expected output: 4.375`} />

The ramp is 4.6 long but only covers 4.375 of floor, because part of its length went into
the climb. [`math.sin()`](/docs/standard-library/math/sin) with the same two values gives
the height it climbed.

### Comparing two headings without working out the angle

<RunnableExample code={`local first_heading = { 3, 4 }\nlocal second_heading = { 5, 12 }\nlocal dot_product = first_heading[1] * second_heading[1] + first_heading[2] * second_heading[2]\nlocal first_length = math.sqrt(first_heading[1] ^ 2 + first_heading[2] ^ 2)\nlocal second_length = math.sqrt(second_heading[1] ^ 2 + second_heading[2] ^ 2)\nlocal agreement = dot_product / (first_length * second_length)\nlocal agreement_threshold = math.cos(math.rad(20))\nprint(string.format("%.4f", agreement))\nprint(agreement > agreement_threshold)\n-- Expected output:\n-- 0.9692\n-- true`} />

That ratio *is* the cosine of the angle between the two headings. Turning the threshold into
a cosine once and comparing against it is cheaper and steadier than turning the measurement
into an angle on every check — and it sidesteps the rounding trap that
[`math.acos()`](/docs/standard-library/math/acos) has to guard against. Note that the
comparison points the other way from the question: "within twenty degrees" becomes "cosine
above `agreement_threshold`", because a wider angle means a *smaller* cosine.

### The sign of the angle makes no difference

<RunnableExample code={`local camera_tilt = math.rad(35)\nprint(math.cos(-camera_tilt) == math.cos(camera_tilt))\nprint(math.sin(-camera_tilt) == math.sin(camera_tilt))\n-- Expected output:\n-- true\n-- false`} />

Tilting one way and the other gives the same cosine and opposite sines. Useful, and a trap:
a cosine on its own cannot say which side of straight ahead something is.

## Gotchas

<Gotcha title="The argument is in radians, and a degree measure is silently wrong">
`math.cos(60)` is not the cosine of sixty degrees. It is the cosine of sixty radians, which
is some other angle entirely, and the call answers with a number in the usual range and
nothing to suggest anything is amiss. Convert with
[`math.rad()`](/docs/standard-library/math/rad) at the point a degree measure enters the
calculation.
</Gotcha>

<Gotcha title="A cosine cannot tell left from right">
Because the sign of the angle is discarded, two directions mirrored across the axis produce
identical cosines, and code that decides "which way do I turn" from a cosine alone will pick
the same way for both. Where the answer must be signed, take it from
[`math.atan()`](/docs/standard-library/math/atan) with two arguments, which keeps the signs
of both components and places the result in the right quadrant.
</Gotcha>
