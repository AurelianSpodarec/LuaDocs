Hands back the tangent of an angle — its sine divided by its cosine, which is the slope a
line at that angle climbs at. The angle is read as a measurement in *radians*, and unlike a
sine or a cosine the answer has no bounds at all.

<RunnableExample code={`local slope_of_thirty = math.tan(math.rad(30))\nprint(slope_of_thirty)\n-- Expected output: 0.57735026918963`} />

## Syntax

```lua
math.tan(x)
```

<Parameters>
  <Param name="x">The angle to take the tangent of, in radians.</Param>
</Parameters>

<Returns>
  <Return type="float">The tangent of `x`. Always a float, whatever `x` was, and of any size.</Return>
</Returns>

<Errors>

- Raises when `x` is neither a number nor a string that reads as one.

</Errors>

## Description

The argument is an angle in radians, so a degree measure has to go through
[`math.rad()`](/docs/standard-library/math/rad) first. A degree measure passed straight in
is answered, not rejected — it is simply the tangent of a different angle.

The value is a *rise over a run*: the tangent of an angle is how far a line at that angle
climbs for every unit it travels sideways. That makes it the function for turning an
observed angle into a distance, and it is why its result is unbounded where a sine and a
cosine are penned between minus one and one. As the angle approaches a quarter turn the run
shrinks toward nothing and the slope grows without limit.

It also repeats twice as often as its neighbours: adding half a turn flips the signs of both
the sine and the cosine, which leaves their ratio alone. So a tangent cannot distinguish an
angle from the same angle turned back on itself — pointing up-and-right gives the same
tangent as pointing down-and-left.

The result is always a float, whatever went in. This function neither mirrors its argument
nor rounds anything, so the library's
[subtype rule](/docs/standard-library/math/floor#which-subtype-comes-back) does not reach it.
The tidy values are no safer to compare against here, for the reason
[set out on `math.sin()`](/docs/standard-library/math/sin#why-the-familiar-values-are-not-exact) —
`math.tan(math.pi / 4) == 1` is one of the comparisons that comes out false.

## Where the tangent has no value

At a quarter turn, and at every half turn from there, the cosine is zero and the tangent has
no value at all. Nothing in this function says so: there is no error there, and no separate
failure result either. The reason is worth knowing, because it is not that the case has been
handled.

A quarter turn is `math.pi / 2`, and no float is exactly that: pi cannot be held in a float,
so `math.pi / 2` is merely very close. The cosine there is therefore very small rather than
zero, the ratio still exists, and what comes back is an enormous but perfectly ordinary finite
number — around ten to the sixteenth. Its digits are noise. They record how far the nearest
float happens to sit from the real quarter turn, so they differ between one Lua build and
another, and no calculation should depend on them.

The practical consequence is that guarding against the pole is pointless: no finite angle
makes this function misbehave in a way a check could catch, only a band of them produce
answers too large to be meaningful. Where a slope is being turned back into an angle, going the other
way with [`math.atan()`](/docs/standard-library/math/atan) avoids the region entirely — every
slope has an angle, even though not every angle has a slope.

## Examples

### The height of something you can only measure the angle of

<RunnableExample code={`local distance_to_tree = 25\nlocal elevation_angle = math.rad(32)\nprint(string.format("%.2f", distance_to_tree * math.tan(elevation_angle)))\n-- Expected output: 15.62`} />

Pace out the distance, measure the angle up to the top, and the tangent turns the pair into
a height. This is the calculation the function is most often reached for.

### The slope runs away near a quarter turn

<RunnableExample code={`for degrees = 80, 89, 3 do\n  print(string.format("%d degrees -> %.3f", degrees, math.tan(math.rad(degrees))))\nend\n-- Expected output:\n-- 80 degrees -> 5.671\n-- 83 degrees -> 8.144\n-- 86 degrees -> 14.301\n-- 89 degrees -> 57.290`} />

Nine degrees of angle multiply the slope by ten. Any code that scales something by a tangent
needs a ceiling on the angle, because the growth arrives faster than a reader expects.

### A quarter turn answers anyway, and answers finitely

<RunnableExample code={`local quarter_turn = math.rad(90)\nlocal steepest_slope = math.tan(quarter_turn)\nprint(steepest_slope > 1e15)\nprint(steepest_slope < math.huge)\n-- Expected output:\n-- true\n-- true`} />

Enormous, and not an infinity — so nothing downstream will notice it as a special value the
way it would notice one. The number itself is not printed here on purpose: its digits are an
artefact of the build.

## Gotchas

<Gotcha title="The argument is in radians, and a degree measure is silently wrong">
`math.tan(45)` is not one. It is the tangent of forty-five radians, and it comes back as an
ordinary number with nothing to mark it as unintended. Convert with
[`math.rad()`](/docs/standard-library/math/rad) where the degree measure enters, since the
result of getting this wrong is a plausible-looking answer rather than a failure.
</Gotcha>

<Gotcha title="Near a quarter turn the answer is huge, finite, and meaningless">
There is no error to catch and no infinity to test for: the call returns a normal float
around ten to the sixteenth, which then multiplies its way through whatever it touches. Code
that computes a length from an angle read off a sensor will happily produce a distance of
astronomical size from an angle a fraction away from vertical. Bound the angle before the
call, not the result after it.
</Gotcha>

<Gotcha title="A tangent cannot tell a direction from its opposite">
Adding half a turn leaves the tangent unchanged, so up-and-right and down-and-left are
indistinguishable once reduced to a slope. Recovering the angle from a slope with a
single-argument [`math.atan()`](/docs/standard-library/math/atan) inherits the ambiguity;
passing the rise and the run as two arguments instead keeps their signs and resolves it.
</Gotcha>
