Divides one number by another and hands back what is left over, having thrown away the
fraction of the quotient rather than rounded it down. That one difference is why it is not
the `%` operator, and why the two part company as soon as the operands have opposite signs.

<RunnableExample code={`local leftover_slices = math.fmod(17, 5)\nprint(leftover_slices)\n-- Expected output: 2`} />

## Syntax

```lua
math.fmod(x, y)
```

<Parameters>
  <Param name="x">The number being divided.</Param>
  <Param name="y">The number to divide it by.</Param>
</Parameters>

<Returns>
  <Return type="integer or float">What is left of `x` after taking away every whole `y` that fits between it and zero. An integer when both arguments are integers, a float when either is a float.</Return>
</Returns>

<Errors>

- <Since v="5.3" /> Raises when both arguments are integers and `y` is zero.
- Raises when either argument is neither a number nor a string that reads as one, and when the call is given fewer than two arguments.

</Errors>

## Description

*One* value comes back, not two — the split into a whole part and a fraction is
[`math.modf()`](/docs/standard-library/math/modf), whose name is one letter away and whose
job is unrelated. This function divides.

The quotient is rounded *toward zero* before the remainder is worked out, which is another
way of saying the fraction of it is discarded rather than rounded in a direction. Two
consequences follow, and they are the whole entry:

- Wherever the division has an answer at all, the result is smaller than `y` in size and
  carries the sign of `x` — the number being divided. Dividing a negative number leaves a
  negative remainder, or none at all, however `y` is signed.
- The `%` [operator](/docs/language/expressions/arithmetic-operators) rounds its quotient
  *down* instead, so its remainder carries the sign of `y`. The two agree whenever `x` and
  `y` share a sign, and whenever the division comes out exactly. Where they disagree they
  disagree by exactly `y`: the operator's answer is this function's answer plus `y`.

Which subtype comes back is the library's mirroring rule rather than this function's own,
and it is
[written out on `math.floor()`](/docs/standard-library/math/floor#which-subtype-comes-back):
two integers give an integer, and one float anywhere gives a float.

A zero `y` is two different situations wearing one face. With two integers there is no
answer to give and the call raises. With a float at either end the arithmetic is
floating-point arithmetic, which has a not-a-number value for exactly this, and that value
is what comes back — silently, with nothing raised.

## Examples

### The sign comes from the number being divided

<RunnableExample code={`local minutes_early = math.fmod(-95, 60)\nlocal minutes_late = math.fmod(95, 60)\nprint(minutes_early)\nprint(minutes_late)\n-- Expected output:\n-- -35\n-- 35`} />

The two results are mirror images, which is what makes this the right choice for a quantity
where the sign is the information — an offset before or after a mark, a drift above or
below a target.

### Fractional operands are ordinary here

<RunnableExample code={`local ribbon_length = 7.5\nlocal piece_length = 2\nprint(math.fmod(ribbon_length, piece_length))\n-- Expected output: 1.5`} />

Three whole pieces come off the ribbon and the offcut is what is handed back. Nothing has
to be a whole number for the division to make sense.

### Where it parts company with the modulo operator

<RunnableExample code={`local offset_degrees = -450\nprint(math.fmod(offset_degrees, 360))\nprint(offset_degrees % 360)\n-- Expected output:\n-- -90\n-- 270`} />

Both answers describe the same direction, and they differ by exactly one full turn.
Whichever is wanted, it has to be chosen: `-90` reads as "ninety short of the mark" and
`270` as "a bearing", and neither is a rounding error in the other.

## Gotchas

<Gotcha title="It is not the modulo operator, and swapping them changes the answer">
They agree on every pair of operands that share a sign, so a substitution made while reading
data of one sign survives every test built from that data. Mix the signs and they part:
`math.fmod(-7, 3)` is `-1` and `-7 % 3` is `2` — the same remainder counted from opposite
ends. Anything that then uses the result as a position, an index or a bucket number lands
somewhere different, or out of range altogether, and only for the half of the input that
crosses zero.
</Gotcha>

<Gotcha title="A zero divisor does not always stop anything">
Dividing by zero raises here only while both arguments are integers. Let a float in at
either end and the same call answers with a not-a-number value instead, which is not equal
to anything including itself, spreads through every arithmetic operation it touches, and
surfaces as a wrong answer far from the division that produced it. Where `y` is computed
rather than written, check it before the call rather than checking the result afterwards.
</Gotcha>
