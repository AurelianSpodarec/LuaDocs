The hyperbolic sine: half the difference between *e* raised to the argument and *e* raised to
its negation. Nothing in the library replaced it, so the way to write it now is the
definition itself — `(math.exp(x) - math.exp(-x)) / 2` — and that subtraction is the one
place a hand-built version parts company with the call it stands in for, near an argument of
zero.

<RunnableExample usesEntry={false} code={`local cable_constant = 40\nlocal horizontal_offset = 25\nlocal scaled_offset = horizontal_offset / cable_constant\n-- math.sinh(scaled_offset) produced this value in one call\nlocal hyperbolic_sine = (math.exp(scaled_offset) - math.exp(-scaled_offset)) / 2\nprint(string.format("%.4f", cable_constant * hyperbolic_sine))\n-- Expected output: 26.6597`} />

<Note>
Nothing on this page calls the function. The examples run on a Lua that does not provide it,
so they build the value out of [`math.exp()`](/docs/standard-library/math/exp) and name the
old call in a comment beside it. The call itself is in the syntax below.
</Note>

## Syntax

```lua
math.sinh (x)
```

<Parameters>
  <Param name="x">The argument, in the same units the exponential takes — a plain number, not an angle.</Param>
</Parameters>

<Returns>
  <Return type="float">The hyperbolic sine of `x`. A float whatever `x` was, carrying the sign of the argument and climbing away from zero in both directions.</Return>
</Returns>

<Errors>

- Raises when `x` is neither a number nor a string that reads as one, including when it is left out.

</Errors>

## Description

The name borrows from trigonometry and the behaviour does not. The circular sine oscillates
between `-1` and `1`; this climbs away from zero in both directions and keeps climbing. Its
best-known appearance is the length of a hanging cable measured out from the lowest point,
which is why the lead example measures one, and it is the odd partner of
[`math.cosh()`](/docs/standard-library/math/cosh) wherever the two turn up together.

Two properties come straight out of the definition. It is *odd*: negating the argument
negates the answer, because negating the argument swaps the two exponentials and so flips the
sign of the difference between them. And it passes through zero at zero, where both
exponentials are `1` and the difference between them is nothing at all.

The answer is a float, and stays one at an argument of zero where it lands exactly on `0`.
Nothing here rounds or mirrors its argument, so the library's
[subtype rule](/docs/standard-library/math/floor#which-subtype-comes-back) does not reach
this call.

Writing the function out by hand carries the general costs set out on
[`math.cosh()`](/docs/standard-library/math/cosh#rebuilding-a-hyperbolic-function-by-hand) —
in short, an intermediate exponential that overflows before the answer would have, and no
error to say so. The special case below belongs to this function alone.

## Where the subtraction costs precision

`math.exp(x)` and `math.exp(-x)` approach each other as the argument approaches zero. Their
difference is what this function is built from, and subtracting two nearly equal floats
cancels the digits they agree on: what survives is what lay underneath, including the
rounding each exponential already carried, so the answer holds fewer significant digits than
either input did. The smaller the argument, the worse the trade.

The behaviour is not dramatic and is easy to overlook, because the answer stays the right
size and only its trailing digits are wrong. At an argument around a thousandth, the loss is
somewhere out past the tenth significant digit and nothing above it notices. At an argument
around a hundred-trillionth, the relative error has reached the fourth digit — visible in the
second example below, where a ratio that should be `1` comes back as `0.9992`.

The rescue is to stop subtracting. For a small argument the hyperbolic sine is very close to
the argument itself, so a series is both faster and more accurate there:

```lua
local hyperbolic_sine = sample_offset + sample_offset * sample_offset * sample_offset / 6
```

Two terms of that are already better than the subtraction across the range where the
subtraction hurts, and switching between the two on a threshold — somewhere around a
thousandth — gives a hand-built version that behaves throughout. Where the argument is never
small, the plain definition is fine and this section can be ignored.

## Examples

### The length of a hanging cable

<RunnableExample usesEntry={false} code={`local cable_constant = 40\nlocal spans = { 10, 25, 40 }\nfor index = 1, #spans do\n  local scaled_offset = spans[index] / cable_constant\n  -- math.sinh(scaled_offset) is what this expression stands in for\n  local hyperbolic_sine = (math.exp(scaled_offset) - math.exp(-scaled_offset)) / 2\n  print(string.format("%d m out: %.4f m of cable", spans[index], cable_constant * hyperbolic_sine))\nend\n-- Expected output:\n-- 10 m out: 10.1045 m of cable\n-- 25 m out: 26.6597 m of cable\n-- 40 m out: 47.0080 m of cable`} />

Ten metres out takes a little over ten metres of cable and forty metres out takes nearly
forty-seven, which is the arc length of the same curve whose height
[`math.cosh()`](/docs/standard-library/math/cosh) describes.

### A small argument, and what the subtraction leaves

<RunnableExample usesEntry={false} code={`local samples = { 0.001, 0.00000000000001 }\nfor index = 1, #samples do\n  local sample_offset = samples[index]\n  -- math.sinh(sample_offset) reached this value without any subtraction\n  local rebuilt = (math.exp(sample_offset) - math.exp(-sample_offset)) / 2\n  print(string.format("%.10f", rebuilt / sample_offset))\nend\n-- Expected output:\n-- 1.0000001667\n-- 0.9992007222`} />

Divided by its own argument, a hyperbolic sine should approach `1` as the argument shrinks.
The first line does. The second is heading the wrong way — the subtraction has eaten three
digits by the time the argument is that small, and the answer is drifting away from the value
it should be converging on.

### The symmetry through zero

<RunnableExample usesEntry={false} code={`local scaled_offset = 1.25\nlocal upward = (math.exp(scaled_offset) - math.exp(-scaled_offset)) / 2\nlocal downward = (math.exp(-scaled_offset) - math.exp(scaled_offset)) / 2\n-- math.sinh(scaled_offset) and math.sinh(-scaled_offset) related the same way\nprint(upward == -downward)\n-- Expected output: true`} />

Exactly opposite rather than approximately so: the two calculations are the same pair of
floats subtracted in opposite orders, and floating-point subtraction is symmetric that way
even when it is inaccurate.

## Gotchas

<Gotcha title="A small argument is where the hand-built version is least accurate">
The cancellation is harmless while the two exponentials differ and ruinous once they nearly
agree, and they nearly agree exactly where the argument is nearly zero — the region a
smoothing or damping calculation spends most of its time in. The answer stays the right size,
so nothing looks wrong; what has gone is the tail of the digits, and it goes silently. Where
the argument can be small, switch to the series above below a threshold.
</Gotcha>

<Gotcha title="This is not a sine of an angle">
The argument is a plain number, not an angle, and the answer is not confined to any range.
Feeding radians in here in the expectation of something between `-1` and `1` gives a number
that grows without limit instead. The shared spelling comes from the algebra rather than from
the two being interchangeable.
</Gotcha>
